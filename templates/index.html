ABOUT

An indigenous detection capability

DeepShield is a final year project built at Mehran University of Engineering and Technology. It detects face-swap and face-reenactment forgeries in video, and reports how confident it is rather than forcing a binary answer.

Why it exists

Synthetic video is now convincing enough to fabricate a public statement, impersonate an official, or manufacture evidence. Existing detection tools are foreign and closed: they cannot be audited, cannot be trusted with sensitive material, and are not maintained against locally relevant threats.

A detector that can be inspected, retrained and run offline is a different kind of asset. That is what this project set out to build.

What makes it work

The backbone is CLIP ViT-L/14. Rather than retraining the whole network, only its LayerNorm parameters are updated — 103,169 of 303 million weights.

This matters more than it sounds. Full fine-tuning teaches a model the specific artefacts of its training set, and it then collapses on forgeries made by any other tool. Preserving the pretrained representation is what keeps cross-dataset accuracy at 0.942 where CNN baselines typically fall to around 0.65.

HOW IT WORKS

Four stages, one verdict

Every upload runs the same pipeline that produced our published benchmark results — no shortcuts between evaluation and deployment.

01

Sample

Up to 32 frames are drawn evenly across the clip, so the whole timeline is covered rather than just the opening seconds.

02

Isolate

Every visible face is detected, rotated so the eyes sit level, and cropped with a 1.3× margin — the region where forgery artefacts concentrate.

03

Classify

A CLIP ViT-L/14 transformer scores each crop. Only its LayerNorm weights were tuned, preserving the representation that lets it generalise.

04

Explain

Attention rollout reveals which facial regions carried the decision, so the verdict arrives with evidence rather than a bare number.

Several faces, one answer

Faces are matched between frames by identity embedding, so each person in a video is tracked and scored separately.

The headline verdict comes from the most suspicious person, not an average. A single manipulated face in an otherwise authentic scene still makes the video manipulated, and averaging across people would hide exactly that case.

When the model is unsure

A score near 50% means the model could not decide — not that the video is halfway real. Those results are labelled inconclusive rather than forced into a verdict.

The interface also reports the full frame-score range, so you can see when the model disagreed with itself across the video instead of reading one averaged number as settled fact.

Compression is a real limit

Video re-encodes every time it is shared. We measured what that costs: accuracy holds near 0.937 down to JPEG quality 60, falls to 0.878 at quality 30, and reaches 0.670 at quality 10.

The app estimates compression from bitrate and warns you when an upload falls into the range where the result should not be trusted.

What it cannot do

The model was trained on face-swap and face-reenactment forgeries. Fully synthetic video from newer generative models is a different problem and may pass as real.

Face tracking uses identity embeddings and can still confuse people who cross paths on screen. Attention maps are a noisy proxy for attribution. This is a research prototype, not forensic evidence.
